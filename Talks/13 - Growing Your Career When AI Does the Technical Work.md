# Growing Your Career When AI Does the Technical Work

**Speaker.** Brittany Ellich, product engineer at Bluesky, previously a staff engineer at GitHub. React Summit, Nov 17.
**How much this matters to you.** High. None of her advice needs code, and one audience question came from someone who works between design and engineering.

## The idea in plain words

Ellich reached staff engineer at GitHub in about two years. She did it by networking, mentoring and leading projects outside her own team. Then she moved to Bluesky, and she got that job because she already knew people in that world.

The market is rough. She cited a 25% drop in front-end roles and said software engineering hires have halved since 2022. Her explanation is that AI filters screen out AI-written resumes, so personal connections are how people get through.

Her main point is about scope. A junior used to own one piece of work, a senior a project, a staff engineer several teams' work. Coding [agents](../Glossary.md#agent), AI tools that write and change code with little hand-holding, now do much of the middle. So every level's job has moved up. Juniors need enough judgment to notice when an agent is "going awry," plus the people skills that used to matter only higher up.

She gave three strategies. Do the work and tell people about it. Make the people around you better. Keep talking to people, one or two real conversations a week. The notes come from a machine transcript of the livestream, so some wording is approximate.

## Why it matters to web specialists

- If you're being asked to ship changes with an AI agent, you're living her point. The typing is cheap now. People notice judgment and relationships.
- She expects human review of [pull requests](../Glossary.md#pull-request), the formal request to add a change to the shared code, to fade. In her words, "those days are probably numbered." [Review](../Glossary.md#code-review) is how people learn what other teams are doing. Designers and SEO people often already carry that knowledge.
- A design system engineer asked how to grow. Her answer was to work out what matters most and do that first. Choosing what to work on shows you can prioritize as well as build.
- 38% of people in LeadDev's 2025 AI Impact survey agreed AI tools have cut the mentoring juniors get. She says anyone who knows something the team doesn't can teach it.

## What you can do with it

These are my suggestions, built on her talk.

- Keep a brag doc, a running list of what you did and why it mattered, somewhere your manager can see it. When you ship something through an agent, write down what you asked for and what changed.
- Tell your manager plainly if you want a promotion. She says most managers won't ask.
- Book short one-on-ones with the engineers whose code your changes touch. Knowing "the person who runs the database" has saved her during incidents.
- If small talk is hard, borrow her opener. "What's your role, and what do you like or dislike about it?"
- If an AI assistant keeps it for you, read what it writes. She uses Claude with [skills](../Glossary.md#skill), saved instructions that teach the AI a repeat task, to fill her brag doc. She admits "sometimes it adds complete junk," like projects she had only thought about.
- Offer to walk the engineers through accessibility or search. By her definition, that's mentoring.

## Words worth knowing

- **Agent.** An AI tool that does multi-step work, such as editing files, without you approving each step.
- **Pull request.** A proposed change that teammates check before it joins the main code.
- **Code review.** A teammate reading your proposed change and asking questions before it goes in.
- **Skill.** A saved set of instructions an AI assistant loads when a certain kind of task comes up.

## A line worth remembering

> "The tools are honestly probably going to keep changing. And the technical part is important, but it's never been the part that gets you further in your career."
> Brittany Ellich
